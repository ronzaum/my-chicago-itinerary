CHICAGO / A PERSONAL PROJECT

How I built my
Chicago itinerary

A little website for a very particular way of spending three days in Chicago. Mine.

Clone the repo ↗

I like staying a little longer in the cities I visit for conferences. Chicago has always been a dream of mine, so I built myself an itinerary to make sure I could fit it all into three days.

I wanted to open my phone and go: the next place, the way there, food nearby and my booking. And somewhere to write down what I noticed.

01

Starting the build

I really hated the first iterations. They weren't well designed for me. They didn't feel like my own thing, they weren't custom enough, and they didn't work with how I wanted to move through the day.

Some looked like magazines. Some looked like travel guides. I didn't need another page about Chicago. I needed something I could use while standing on a pavement, looking for the next thing.

02

Making it mine

The design wasn't a finishing touch. It was a huge part of the project. I kept changing the colour, spacing, type, controls and tickets until the whole thing felt like something I had chosen.

Colour, very deliberately

I chose these colours myself, through lots of comparisons. Blue came from Virgil Abloh's Figures of Speech book. Yellow came from Frank Lloyd Wright's stained glass. Cherry was my take on Mies's steel and OMA's McCormick Tribune Campus Center at IIT.

BlueYellowCherry

I wasn't just choosing swatches. I was choosing what the colour did: which surface held the date, which marked a meal, which made a ticket visible and how the same day felt at night.

Yellow took a lot of work. When I asked for less green, I didn't mean less yellow. Later, ripe bananas became a useful reference: the dark navigation and booking controls needed to belong to that same colour family, rather than drifting into walnut.

Comparing whole systems, not random colours

I explored muted families, monochrome directions, richer plum and cream combinations, brown and lavender, mixed palettes and later green refinements. Sometimes I was comparing a colour. Other times I was comparing where that colour belonged across the entire interface. Those were different decisions.

A dark heading on a pale page felt different from a whole page filled with that same dark colour. I compared the day heading, navigation, sightseeing rows, practical stops, meals and tickets together. I also wanted each day to remain recognisable when I switched between light and dark mode.

I kept the original beside the alternatives. When I still preferred its day-heading yellow, I brought that part back without throwing away every other refinement. At another point I had to clarify exactly which dark version I meant. Once there were several branches, “the dark one” stopped being a useful baseline.

The personal itinerary eventually used Bottle Green for Thursday, Cherry for Friday, Yellow for Saturday and Electric Blue for Sunday. Thursday went through its own refinement: no gold, subtler light rows, and the original Deep Jewel green. I also gave meal and practical rows their own approved shades so they could be quieter than sightseeing without losing their place in the day.

The rule became exact: use the approved colour and its matching text colour. Don't invent another shade because a surface needs to feel softer. Tickets needed their own fill role, separate from the row around them. Light mode and dark mode both had to preserve that hierarchy.

Letters with a personality

I wanted a childish, rounded title without making the whole interface childish to read. I compared wordmarks, adjusted the open spaces inside letters and kept the body text simple. The title could be expressive; the time and address needed to be clear.

Wordmark optionsType & densityHeader controls

I wanted the words themselves to be the logo. The first logo directions missed that: I was talking about the actual title in the header. Once the bubbly direction was right, I compared size, weight and the open spaces inside the letters.

The final choice combined the larger size of option 6 with the more open counters of option 5. My local DynaPuff derivative, Trip Puff Open, enlarges the inside spaces of A and R by 30% horizontally and 20% vertically, while retaining the other letter shapes and advance widths. It wasn't just extra spacing between letters.

Small buttons, lots of decisions

The pencil needed to look like a pencil. Inventory became four simple blocks. Map, directions and tickets needed enough room to tap, but they couldn't compete with the stop title. I kept moving between the complete screen and the individual control: a button that looked good on its own could still be too loud in a full day.

I reduced the meal disclosure to an icon, a count and a chevron, while keeping an accessible name. The actions stayed beside the stop rather than in a detached toolbar. The outlines needed to be subtle, the ticket needed to remain recognisable, and the row still needed to read as one unit.

Tickets that feel like tickets

I tried cutouts, perforations, denser details, bigger graphics and different separators. I liked the paper-ticket feeling, but I didn't want the decoration to bury what I needed at the entrance.

Early paper-ticket optionsDenser ticket optionsFinal paper-ticket direction

I eventually preferred an earlier direction. Keeping the alternatives meant I could return to the shape I liked and retain the current information. The final hierarchy is simple: what the booking is, when it is, its status and the original document action.

I also tested how much booking information belonged on the face of each ticket, the title treatment, the cloud and document controls, and how confirmed bookings should sit apart from things still to do. The separator itself went through options before I selected a perforated treatment.

I removed an unnecessary white header band and simplified the Back action. I wanted compact, consistent metadata and clear statuses—not more decoration every time I asked for a refinement. The colour belonged to the booking's date, even when I opened it from another day.

Returning to an earlier design was a choice, not a reason to lose the newer booking facts. I wanted the shape I preferred with the current content and working original-document actions.

More iterations didn't always mean more detail. Sometimes they helped me remove it.

03

Everything next to
the thing it belongs to

The connections mattered as much as the screens. I wanted the button where I would need it, with the right context already attached.

The action belongs to the moment: directions when I’m leaving, nearby choices when I’m hungry, and the original booking when I arrive. I wanted each tap to carry the place or meal I had already chosen.

The journal was its own little space

I didn't want the journal to feel like a small form squeezed into an inventory page. I adjusted its width, removed the framing and made the writing area reach the page edges, with a small text inset. The bird and pencil belonged to the same personal world as the title, but writing still needed to be easy and quiet.

There were two different ideas of “saved” in my private version: a recoverable draft while I typed, and a submitted reflection. I wanted a failed save to leave my words available. A retry should not create a second copy just because the first response was lost. Location could be captured once when sending, if permission allowed it; it wasn't meant to track me continuously.

The submitted entries remain sealed behind a manual gate. Reading them later is a separate part of the journal's story. The public demo uses browser-local saving, so that private workflow isn't something a visitor silently inherits.

The map needed the right context

I moved from an earlier Leaflet/OpenStreetMap-related implementation to Google Maps. Opening the map and opening directions remained separate actions: one helps me understand the places inside the itinerary, the other gets me moving.

One correction was particularly important: the map inside breakfast needed to show that breakfast's alternatives, not every food option for the day. The wider food map remained useful, but it was a different action. I wanted the selected date, meal and destination to survive the transition between screens.

For food research I took a self-contained prompt to Gemini and Muse, then brought the reports back. The useful context was each meal's location, timing, next stop and inexpensive alternatives. I wanted useful choices, not a rigid appointment for every meal.

These small decisions made it personal: space for a seated break, alternatives without a crowded day, a familiar icon instead of another label, and light and dark modes that carry through the whole experience.

04

Building, checking,
changing my mind

I used AI coding tools to build and revise the site. The work lived in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with Google Maps for maps and external directions links. I kept project decisions in AGENTS.md and PROJECT_MEMORY.md, and used project workflows such as /explore, /create-plan and /execute to move from a question to a concrete change.

The most useful instructions named the baseline, the exact thing to change and what should stay fixed. “Make it better” was much less useful than pointing to the navigation band, the ticket cutouts or the width of the journal.

I checked more than a single screenshot: different days, both themes, opening and closing panels, following actions and reloading. A good-looking desktop preview still isn't proof of how it behaves on an actual iPhone.

Safari draft recovery exposed a real problem: a browser check hadn't been enough. I had to test recovery again on my phone after the storage handling and cached modules changed. The coloured band at the top of Safari had its own round of corrections. Those details mattered because I was designing something to open on my iPhone, not just something to screenshot on a laptop.

I kept the exploration and implementation workflows separate: inspect the existing project, define a bounded change, then implement and check it. Some of these skills—including the explore, create-plan and execute workflows—originally came from Zevi Arnovitz. I edited them for my own project; I did not build them from scratch. They were installed project workflows, not commands every reader automatically has. Keeping accepted choices in the project made it easier to preserve them while continuing to experiment.

The public version removes my private travel details. It keeps the interface available to explore and the source available to adapt. Its journal saves locally in the browser; someone building their own version needs their own Maps configuration and document links.

I haven't finished the trip yet. This is the tool I'm getting ready to use—and the design process that made it feel like mine.
